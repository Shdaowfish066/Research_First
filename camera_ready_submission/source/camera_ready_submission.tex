\begin{abstract}
Choosing activation functions for gated recurrent classifiers
requires distinguishing differences between configurations from
variation across training runs. We evaluate 100 ordered pairs
of ten activation functions in two dense layers across four
LSTM/GRU architectures on IMDb. Each configuration is trained
with five seeds on a fixed data split, giving 2{,}000 runs.
Variation across seeds accounts for 58.7\% of total
test-accuracy variance, compared with 26.7\% for architecture,
3.7\% for activation pairs and 10.9\% for their interaction.
GRU exceeds LSTM by 0.79 percentage points in family mean
accuracy and has about 40\% lower median standard deviation
across seeds. Single-run and five-seed rankings show little
agreement: Spearman correlations range from $-0.174$ to
$+0.055$. The ten highest configuration means span only
0.124 percentage points, less than the seed standard deviation
of nine of those configurations. A separate early-learning
experiment shows differences in epoch-one validation accuracy,
while validation losses become closely grouped by epoch three.
Under these IMDb settings, architecture differences are clearer
than the ordering of leading activation pairs. The findings
support reporting both mean accuracy and variation across seeds
when comparing activation choices.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In recurrent sentiment classifiers, the activation functions
in the dense layers help turn the learned text representation
into a prediction. They can be changed without altering the
internal gates of LSTM or GRU cells
\cite{hochreiter1997,cho2014}. Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2025}
compared three activation configurations in LSTM-based IMDb
classifiers. Such comparisons raise a practical question:
how much does dense-layer activation choice matter relative
to architecture and variation between training runs?

A single accuracy score cannot separate these effects.
Weight initialisation, dropout masks and batch order can
change the score even when the model configuration and data
stay fixed. A pair that ranks first in one run may therefore
have a lower average accuracy over repeated training.
Assessing activation choices requires comparing their mean
performance with the variation observed across seeds.

We test all 100 ordered pairs of ten activation functions in
two dense layers across four architectures on IMDb
\cite{maas2011}. Five seeds per configuration give
2{,}000 runs on the same data split. The study measures
architecture and activation effects alongside seed variation,
compares original single-run rankings with five-seed rankings,
and examines early learning in a separate follow-up. This
design addresses both average performance and consistency
across training runs through six research questions:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} How much accuracy variation comes from
        architecture, activation choice and training seeds?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} Which architectures achieve higher mean
        test accuracy?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} How do accuracy gaps between leading
        activation pairs compare with variation across seeds?
  \item \textbf{RQ4:} Do single-run rankings agree with
        five-seed rankings?
  \item \textbf{RQ5:} How does activation choice affect early
        learning?
  \item \textbf{RQ6:} Which architectures give more consistent
        test accuracy across training runs?
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

LSTM and GRU networks use gates to control how information
is retained across a sequence
\cite{hochreiter1997,cho2014,chung2014}. Bidirectional
recurrent networks process the sequence in both directions
\cite{schuster1997}. These architectural choices determine
the representation passed to the classifier, while the
activation functions in subsequent dense layers provide
another design choice.

Studies of activation choice differ in where the functions
are applied. Farzad et al.~\cite{farzad2019} compared
23 functions within an LSTM on IMDb, Movie Review and MNIST.
Essai Ali et al.~\cite{essaiAli2022} examined alternatives
to the state activation inside LSTM cells. Eger et
al.~\cite{eger2018} compared 21 functions across eight NLP
tasks and also tested replacements for LSTM gate and state
activations. Their sentence-classification experiments
averaged five random weight initializations, so repeated
training is already part of this literature.

On IMDb, Zhu and Tan~\cite{zhu2022} evaluated a proposed
activation in simple RNN, LSTM and GRU models. Ahmed et
al.~\cite{ahmed2025} studied three LSTM-based configurations
with different activation pairs and reported accuracies of
88.3--88.4\%. Their focus on the dense layers is closely
related to ours, whereas changing internal recurrent
activations changes a different part of the model.

Our study evaluates both dense-layer activations jointly
while keeping internal recurrent activations unchanged.
The same set of ordered pairs is tested across four
architectures under shared training settings. Repeating every
configuration allows us to compare architecture and activation
differences with seed variation and to measure how rankings
change after averaging runs. The contribution is this
controlled comparison of effect sizes, ranking agreement and
consistency within the IMDb setting.

\section{Methodology}

We compare architecture and activation choices using repeated
training on the same IMDb data. The main experiment tests
400 configurations with five seeds each, giving 2{,}000 runs.
It measures mean test accuracy, variation across seeds and
changes in activation rankings. A separate experiment examines
learning during the first training epochs.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

The IMDb dataset contains 50,000 reviews with balanced positive
and negative labels~\cite{maas2011}. We use a stratified 80/20
split with seed 42, reserving 10,000 reviews for testing.
Of the remaining 40,000, 4,000 are withheld for validation
and 36,000 are used for training. All runs use these same sets.

Reviews are lowercased after removing HTML tags and
non-alphabetic characters. We build a 5,000-token vocabulary,
including padding and unknown-word tokens, from the 40,000
reviews before withholding validation data. Reviews are
truncated or zero-padded to 200 tokens
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}).

\subsection{Architectures and Activation Functions}

We compare four architectures formed by choosing LSTM or GRU
cells and a unidirectional or bidirectional second recurrent
layer (Table~\ref{tab:architectures}). The first recurrent
layer is always bidirectional, and both layers use the same
cell type.

Each model uses a 128-dimensional embedding, recurrent layers
with 64 and 32 units per direction, dropout (0.4), dense
layers with 64 and 32 units, and one output unit. The final
hidden state of the second recurrent layer feeds the dense
layers; both directional states are concatenated when that
layer is bidirectional.
Fig.~\ref{fig:model_architecture} shows the layer sequence,
tensor shapes and the two activation functions varied in the sweep.

\subsection{Activation Sweep and Repeated Training}
\label{sec:replication}

Each of the ten functions is tested with all ten functions
in the other dense layer, including itself. This gives
100 ordered pairs per architecture and 400 configurations
in total. Every configuration is trained with seeds
$\{10,20,30,40,50\}$, producing 500 runs per architecture
and 2{,}000 overall.

The training seed controls weight initialisation, dropout
and batch order. We seed Python, NumPy, PyTorch and CUDA,
enable deterministic cuDNN operation and disable cuDNN
benchmarking. The data split and vocabulary stay fixed,
so the repeated runs measure variation from training the
same configuration on the same data.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation}

Training settings are shared across the main sweep
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). We use Adam and binary
cross-entropy with logits for at most ten epochs.
Validation loss controls learning-rate reduction and early
stopping. We restore the model with the lowest validation
loss before evaluating test accuracy, precision, recall and
F1. Positive sentiment is the positive class; predictions
use a sigmoid probability threshold of 0.5.

\subsection{Accuracy, Variation and Ranking Comparisons}

We calculate each configuration's mean accuracy and sample
standard deviation (SD) across five seeds. Architecture means
average 500 runs, and family means average 1{,}000. Median
configuration SDs summarize consistency within each architecture
or family. To compare individual first-layer functions, we
average each over its ten second-layer partners and five seeds
(50 runs per mean).

We partition the total squared deviations from the overall
accuracy mean into architecture, activation pair, their
interaction and variation across seeds within configurations.
Each part is reported as a percentage of the total
(Table~\ref{tab:variance}).

Within each architecture, we rank the 100 pairs by their
original single-run accuracy and by their five-seed means.
Spearman correlation measures agreement between these rankings;
mean absolute rank change and top-ten overlap show how much
the ordering changes (Table~\ref{tab:reproducibility}).

\subsection{Early Learning}
\label{sec:convergence_protocol}

To examine early learning, we train BiGRU+GRU with each of
the ten activation functions used in both dense layers,
using seeds $\{10,20,30,40,50\}$. These 50 additional runs
use the same data and training hyperparameters as the main
sweep. The follow-up seeds PyTorch and NumPy and does not
modify cuDNN flags. It records validation accuracy and loss
after each epoch without evaluating the test set.
Results are averaged across the five seeds
(Section~\ref{sec:learning}, Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence_curves}).

\subsection{Baseline Comparison}
\label{sec:baseline_repro}

The embedding, layer sizes and dropout rate follow
Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2025}, but we apply dropout after
the recurrent layers rather than within them. We specify
our own training settings because their training details
are incomplete. Their reported 88.40\% is a reference,
not an exact reproduction target under these settings.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Variation in Test Accuracy}
\label{sec:variance}

Variation across the five seeds within each configuration
accounts for \textbf{58.7\%} of total test-accuracy variance,
the largest component (Table~\ref{tab:variance}). Architecture
accounts for 26.7\%, and activation pairs for 3.7\%. Seed
variation is therefore about sixteen times the activation-pair
component. A further 10.9\% comes from the architecture--activation
interaction: differences between activation pairs change
across architectures.

Within the architecture component, the choice between LSTM
and GRU accounts for 26.3\% of total variance, compared with
0.19\% for making the second recurrent layer bidirectional.

\subsection{Classification Performance}
\label{sec:classification}

Both GRU architectures have higher mean accuracy than both
LSTM architectures (Table~\ref{tab:sweep_summary}). Each

\subsection{The Ten Highest Mean Accuracies}

All ten leading configurations use GRU, as do the top fifty
of the 400 tested configurations. ReLU + LeakyReLU in
BiGRU+GRU has the highest five-seed mean at 87.81\%, with
an SD of 0.17 percentage points (Fig.~\ref{fig:top_configs}).
The highest individual accuracy among all 2{,}000 runs is
88.39\%.

The ten means range from 87.68\% to 87.81\%, spanning
0.124 percentage points before rounding. Their seed SDs
range from 0.10 to 0.40 points. For nine of the ten
configurations, the SD exceeds the entire span of the ten
means. Variation between runs therefore often exceeds the mean
gaps; this comparison alone does not establish statistical
significance.

\subsection{Agreement Between Single-Seed and Five-Seed Rankings}

Within each architecture, we ranked the 100 activation pairs
by their original single-run accuracy and by their five-seed
mean. All four original winners rank 77th or lower by five-seed mean.
Table~\ref{tab:original_winners} compares their original
accuracies with their repeated-run means.

\input{tables/tab8_original_winners.tex}

Across all 100 pairs, Spearman rank correlations range from
$-0.174$ to $+0.055$, indicating little agreement
(Table~\ref{tab:reproducibility}). Pairs move an average of
32.1--36.9 places, and each architecture's two top-ten lists
have at most one pair in common. The mean absolute difference
between original accuracy and five-seed mean is 0.34--0.53
percentage points.

\input{tables/tab8_reproducibility.tex}

For example, LeakyReLU + Tanh leads the original BiGRU+GRU
sweep at 88.05\%, but ranks 77th with a five-seed mean of
87.39\%. Its repeated accuracies range from 86.70\% to
88.20\%, with an SD of 0.55 points. Its original leading
score was therefore not representative of its average
performance across the five repeated runs.

\subsection{Mean Accuracy for Each Activation Function}

Within each architecture, we average test accuracy for each
first-layer activation over the ten second-layer functions
and five seeds (50 runs). Table~\ref{tab:activation_means}
shows the highest and lowest of these ten function means.

\input{tables/tab7_activation_summary.tex}

The largest gap is 0.67 percentage points in BiLSTM+LSTM,
compared with 0.18--0.21 points in the other architectures.
ReLU has the highest mean in both LSTM architectures;
PReLU leads in BiGRU+GRU and SiLU in BiGRU+BiGRU. Thus,
the function with the highest observed mean depends on the
architecture. These means average over partner functions
and do not identify the best individual pair.

\subsection{Learning During Training}
\label{sec:learning}

Across the ten matching activation pairs, epoch-one mean
validation accuracy ranges from 58.78\% for SELU to 71.42\%
for SiLU, a gap of 12.64 percentage points. Each mean covers
five seeds; the median accuracy SD across the ten pairs is
6.70 points (Section~\ref{sec:convergence_protocol}).

Mean validation losses narrow from 0.566--0.670 after one
epoch to 0.291--0.304 by epoch three
(Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence_curves}). Activation choice changes
early validation performance, but the loss curves become
closely grouped within three epochs.

\subsection{Variation Between Runs}
\label{sec:seed_variation}

GRU configurations have lower median accuracy SDs in both
architectures (Table~\ref{tab:sweep_summary}). Pooling the
200 configuration SDs within each family gives medians of
0.30 percentage points for GRU and 0.50 for LSTM, about
\textbf{40\% lower} for GRU.

All eight configurations with an SD above one point use
LSTM. Hardswish + LeakyReLU in BiLSTM+LSTM has the largest
SD (9.27 points) and a mean of 82.06\%, below the plotted
range in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_spread}. Its five runs
range from 65.50\% to 86.85\%, including the lowest accuracy
among all 2{,}000 runs. This wide range shows why a
configuration's mean alone does not describe its consistency.

For each configuration, we also compare precision and recall
after averaging each metric across five seeds. The largest
absolute gap is 6.89 percentage points, compared with 24.17
points for individual runs. Averaging can therefore hide larger gaps in individual runs.

The reported mean training time is 13.4~s per run on an
NVIDIA RTX 5080. BiGRU+GRU has about 734K parameters,
compared with 764K for BiLSTM+LSTM
(Table~\ref{tab:architectures}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: How much accuracy variation comes from architecture, activation choice and training seeds?}
\label{sec:rq1}

Seed variation dominates at 58.7\%, followed by architecture
(26.7\%), the architecture--activation interaction (10.9\%)
and activation pairs (3.7\%; Table~\ref{tab:variance}).
The interaction shows that observed activation-pair gaps
depend on the architecture.

\subsection{RQ2: Which architectures achieve higher mean test accuracy?}
\label{sec:rq2}

Both GRU architectures have higher mean test accuracy than
both LSTM architectures, with a family mean gap of 0.79
percentage points (Table~\ref{tab:sweep_summary}). Within
this setup, choosing the recurrent cell type produces a
larger mean accuracy difference than making the second
recurrent layer bidirectional.

\subsection{RQ3: How do accuracy gaps between leading activation pairs compare with variation across seeds?}
\label{sec:rq3}

The ten leading means span 0.124 percentage points, less
than the seed SD of nine of those configurations
(Fig.~\ref{fig:top_configs}). Their ordering therefore
provides limited evidence for choosing one pair as consistently
best. This does not establish that the pairs perform equally.

\subsection{RQ4: Do single-run rankings agree with five-seed rankings?}
\label{sec:rq4}

The rankings show little agreement, with at most one pair
shared by the original and five-seed top-ten lists in each
architecture (Table~\ref{tab:reproducibility}). This affects
configuration selection: pairs favored by one run can differ
from those with the highest means after repeated training.

\subsection{RQ5: How does activation choice affect early learning?}
\label{sec:rq5}

Activation choice affects epoch-one validation accuracy
(Section~\ref{sec:learning}), but validation losses are
closely grouped by epoch three (Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence_curves}).
An advantage early in training does not establish higher
final test accuracy.

\subsection{RQ6: Which architectures give more consistent test accuracy across training runs?}
\label{sec:rq6}

GRU's higher mean accuracy comes with lower seed variation:
its pooled median SD is 0.30 percentage points, compared
with 0.50 for LSTM, about 40\% lower
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep_summary}, Section~\ref{sec:seed_variation}).

\subsection{Limitations}

The study uses one dataset and a fixed data split. It does
not measure variation caused by changing the split or
establish whether the activation patterns hold on other
datasets. 

\section{Conclusion}

We evaluate 100 ordered dense-layer activation pairs across
four recurrent architectures on IMDb, with five seeds per
configuration, giving 2{,}000 runs. Seed variation accounts for 58.7\% of
total variance, architecture for 26.7\%, activation pairs for
3.7\% and their interaction for 10.9\%
(Table~\ref{tab:variance}). This design measures differences between model choices
alongside variation from retraining the same configuration.

GRU combines higher average accuracy with more consistent
training results. Its family mean exceeds LSTM by 0.79
percentage points (Table~\ref{tab:sweep_summary}), and its
median SD across seeds is about 40\% lower
(Section~\ref{sec:seed_variation}). Together, these results
favor GRU within the tested setup. They provide a clearer
basis for architecture choice than the small mean gaps
provide for ordering the leading activation pairs.

Repeated training also changes which activation pairs appear
strongest. The original and five-seed rankings show little
agreement (Table~\ref{tab:reproducibility}), while gaps among
the leading means are small relative to their seed SDs
(Fig.~\ref{fig:top_configs}). Repeated training therefore changes which configurations
would be selected from their observed scores. These results establish
neither a consistently best activation pair nor equal
performance among pairs. Activation choice also affects
early learning, although validation losses become closely
grouped by epoch three (Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence_curves}).

Reporting mean accuracy together with variation across seeds
makes the basis for configuration choice more transparent.
In this study, the evidence supports GRU's combination of
higher mean accuracy and lower seed variation, while the
ordering of leading activation pairs remains uncertain.
These conclusions apply to the tested architectures and
training settings on a fixed IMDb split.
