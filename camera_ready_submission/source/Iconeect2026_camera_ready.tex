\begin{abstract}
Activation-function comparisons in gated recurrent networks are
almost always reported from a single training run. It has therefore
never been established whether the differences they report are
larger than the noise of retraining the same model. This paper
settles that question at scale. We train all 100 ordered pairs of ten
activation functions in the dense layers of four gated recurrent
architectures on IMDb, and repeat every one of the 400
configurations under five random seeds, for \textbf{2{,}000 models}
in total with every other setting held fixed. A variance
decomposition over these runs shows that the random seed accounts
for \textbf{58.7\%} of all variation in test accuracy and the
architecture for \textbf{26.7\%}, while the activation pair explains
only \textbf{3.7\%}. The consequence is direct: single-seed
activation rankings do not reproduce. Across the four
architectures the Spearman correlation between the single-seed
ranking of the 100 pairs and their five-seed means is
$-0.174$, $-0.059$, $-0.041$ and $+0.055$, indistinguishable from
zero in every case, and the ten best configurations overlap within
their confidence intervals. What survives is architectural: GRU
outperforms LSTM by 0.79 points ($p<10^{-100}$, Cohen's $d=1.19$),
supplies all fifty highest-scoring configurations, and is the more
reliable choice, with roughly 40\% lower seed-to-seed standard
deviation. One activation effect also survives: smooth
functions reach 73.7\% validation accuracy after one epoch against
61.1\% for the ReLU family. We give the seed budget needed to
resolve each remaining effect, and argue that grid searches of this
kind should report variance as standard.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sentiment analysis is the task of deciding whether a piece of
text is positive or negative. It is one of the most common
problems in natural language processing. The IMDb Large
Movie Review Dataset, with 50{,}000 balanced reviews, is the
standard benchmark for the binary version of the
task~\cite{maas2011}. LSTM and GRU networks are the usual
choice for this problem because their gating mechanisms let
them track context across long sequences~\cite{hochreiter1997,cho2014,chung2014}.

One part of these models gets little attention: the activation
function in the dense layers that sit after the recurrent
layers. The gates inside the recurrent cell are fixed to
sigmoid and tanh, but the dense layers that turn the recurrent
output into a final prediction can use any activation function.
Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2025} tested three classical
combinations in a bidirectional LSTM, reached 88.40\% accuracy,
and asked for follow-up work on modern activation functions and
other architectures.

Answering that call runs into a problem. Every comparison we are
aware of, including all of
\cite{eger2018,farzad2019,essaiAli2022,zhu2022,ahmed2025}, trains
each configuration once. Training is not deterministic. The weight
initialisation, the dropout masks and the order of the training
batches all change the final accuracy, even when the data and every
setting are held identical. If that change is as large as the gaps
between activation functions, then a ranking built from one run per
function is measuring the seed rather than the function. No study
in this area reports how large that change is, so there is no way
to tell which is being measured.

We therefore designed this study around replication. We train all
100 ordered pairs of ten activation functions across four
architectures (LSTM, GRU, and the fully bidirectional version of
each), then repeat every one of those 400 configurations under five
random seeds, holding the data split fixed so that only
initialisation, dropout and batch order vary. The result is
\textbf{2{,}000 trained models}. To our knowledge this is the
largest controlled activation study in gated recurrent networks,
and the first in this setting to report seed variance. Five runs of
every configuration let us measure how much accuracy moves when
only the seed changes, and compare that against the gaps between
activation functions:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} How is accuracy variance divided between
        architecture, activation choice and seed?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} Does the architecture exceed seed noise?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} Does the activation pair exceed seed noise?
  \item \textbf{RQ4:} Does a single-seed ranking reproduce?
  \item \textbf{RQ5:} Which activation effects survive?
  \item \textbf{RQ6:} How many seeds would the rest require?
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber~\cite{hochreiter1997} introduced
the LSTM to address the vanishing gradient problem in
recurrent networks, using sigmoid and tanh within the
recurrent cell. Cho et al.~\cite{cho2014} proposed the GRU,
which uses two gates and combines the cell and hidden
states. Chung et al.~\cite{chung2014} compared GRU and LSTM
on sequence-modeling tasks and found GRU competitive with
LSTM. Schuster and Paliwal~\cite{schuster1997} introduced
bidirectional recurrent networks, allowing models to use
both past and future context. These studies established
the recurrent architectures used in our comparison.

The activation function literature developed alongside
these architectural advances. Nair and Hinton~\cite{nair2010}
studied ReLU, which avoids saturation for positive inputs
but has zero gradients for negative inputs. Maas et
al.~\cite{maas2013} and Clevert et al.~\cite{clevert2015}
studied LeakyReLU and ELU, which allow nonzero outputs for
negative inputs. Klambauer et al.~\cite{klambauer2017}
introduced SELU with self-normalising properties under
specific conditions. Later work introduced
GELU~\cite{hendrycks2016}, Swish~\cite{ramachandran2017},
and Mish~\cite{misra2020}, while
surveys~\cite{nwankpa2018,apicella2021} reviewed the growing
range of activation functions. These developments motivate
examining their effects in the dense layers following
gated recurrent networks.

Within sentiment analysis, IMDb~\cite{maas2011} is a widely
used binary classification benchmark. Tang et
al.~\cite{tang2015} studied gated recurrent networks for
document-level sentiment classification, and Socher et
al.~\cite{socher2013} introduced the Stanford Sentiment
Treebank. Several studies have examined activation choice.
Eger et al.~\cite{eger2018} compared 21 activations across
eight NLP tasks and also investigated alternative
activations within LSTM cells. Farzad et
al.~\cite{farzad2019} compared 23 activation functions in
a basic LSTM network on IMDb, Movie Review, and MNIST.
Essai Ali et al.~\cite{essaiAli2022} evaluated 26 state
activation functions in LSTM networks on classification
tasks outside sentiment analysis. Zhu and
Tan~\cite{zhu2022} tested a custom activation in simple RNN,
LSTM, and GRU models on IMDb, comparing it with ReLU,
LeakyReLU, ELU, SELU, and tanh. Ahmed et
al.~\cite{ahmed2025} compared three classical activation
pairings in a bidirectional LSTM, reported 88.40\% accuracy,
and suggested examining modern functions and other
architectures.

Our study brings these comparisons into a shared experimental
design. We evaluate all 100 ordered pairs of ten activation
functions in two dense layers across four architectures.
These use LSTM or GRU cells, a bidirectional first recurrent
layer, and either a unidirectional or bidirectional second
layer. The comparison includes GELU, SiLU, and Mish, with
the data split and training settings held fixed.

Repeated training is also part of earlier work. Eger et
al.~\cite{eger2018} explicitly averaged results over five
random weight initializations in their sentence-classification
experiments. Our main contribution is therefore the analysis
of how variation between training runs compares with the
effects of architecture and dense-layer activation choice
in this setting. We train every configuration with five
seeds and examine whether rankings from one run agree with
rankings based on repeated runs. This allows us to assess
which performance differences are clear and which remain
uncertain.%heyyy

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

The IMDb Large Movie Review Dataset~\cite{maas2011}
contains 50{,}000 reviews with balanced positive and negative
labels. We divided the dataset into 80\% training data and
20\% test data using \texttt{random\_state=42}. We then
reserved 10\% of the training portion for validation,
giving 36{,}000 training, 4{,}000 validation, and
10{,}000 test reviews.

Reviews were cleaned by removing HTML tags and
non-alphabetic characters, then converting the text to
lowercase. The vocabulary contained the 5{,}000 most
frequent tokens in the training set. Each review was
truncated or zero-padded to a length of 200 tokens.

\subsection{Network Architecture}

All models followed the same overall structure: an embedding
layer, two recurrent layers, a dropout layer, two dense layers,
and a single output unit. The embedding layer used a vocabulary
of 5{,}000 tokens and produced 128-dimensional vectors.
The first recurrent layer was bidirectional, with 64 units
per direction. The second recurrent layer contained
32 units per direction and was either unidirectional
or bidirectional.

The recurrent layers were followed by dropout with a rate
of 0.4. The two dense layers contained 64 and 32 units,
with activation functions denoted by Act$_1$ and Act$_2$,
respectively. The output layer produced a single logit,
and the model was trained using \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss}.

We varied two architectural features: the recurrent cell
type, LSTM or GRU, and the directionality of the second
recurrent layer. This produced the four architectures
listed in Table~\ref{tab:architectures}. Within each
architecture, we evaluated every ordered combination
of Act$_1$ and Act$_2$.

\subsection{Activation Functions}

We evaluated ten activation functions:
ReLU~\cite{nair2010},
LeakyReLU~\cite{maas2013},
PReLU~\cite{he2015},
ELU~\cite{clevert2015},
tanh,
SELU~\cite{klambauer2017},
GELU~\cite{hendrycks2016},
SiLU (Swish)~\cite{ramachandran2017},
Mish~\cite{misra2020}, and
Hardswish~\cite{howard2019}.
Table~\ref{tab:activations} summarises their main properties.

Each function was tested in both dense-layer positions,
giving $10\times10=100$ ordered pairs per architecture.
Only the dense-layer activations were changed; the
activations within the recurrent cells remained fixed.

\subsection{Training Configuration and Evaluation}

All configurations used the training settings listed in
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}. Within each architecture,
only the activation pair and training seed varied.
The vocabulary size, sequence length, embedding dimension,
and layer sizes followed the corresponding settings
described by Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2025}. We specified
the optimiser, learning rate, batch size, scheduling,
and gradient clipping for our experiments.

Models were trained with Adam at an initial learning rate
of $10^{-3}$ and a batch size of 64, for a maximum of
10 epochs. Early stopping used a patience of three epochs
based on validation loss. A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler
reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.5, and the
$L_2$ norm of the gradients was clipped to 1.0.

After training, each model was evaluated on the held-out
test set using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Replication Protocol}
\label{sec:replication}

The experiment combined four architectures with all
100 ordered activation pairs, giving 400 configurations.
Each configuration was trained with five random seeds,
$\{10,20,30,40,50\}$, producing 2{,}000 models in the
main experiment.

Before constructing each model and its data loaders,
we set the random seed for \texttt{random},
\texttt{numpy}, \texttt{torch}, and \texttt{torch.cuda}.
We also enabled \texttt{cudnn.deterministic=True}.
Five runs were executed concurrently, with one process
per seed. To check reproducibility in our computing
environment, we repeated selected configurations
sequentially and obtained identical evaluation metrics.

The data split remained fixed at
\texttt{random\_state=42} throughout the experiment.
The reported variation therefore reflects repeated
training on the same data split and does not include
variation caused by changing the split.

We analysed test accuracy across the 2{,}000 runs to
examine the effects of architecture, Act$_1$, Act$_2$,
and the interaction between the two activation choices.
Variation between repeated runs was measured within
each architecture--activation configuration and pooled
across configurations.

% Author check: State the exact sum-of-squares decomposition

To examine convergence, we conducted an additional
50 runs using the ten pairs in which both dense layers
used the same activation function (X+X). Each pair was
trained on BiGRU+GRU with five seeds, with validation
metrics recorded after every epoch. This comparison
measured learning behaviour when the same activation
was used in both dense layers.

\subsection{Comparison with Ahmed et al.}
\label{sec:baseline_repro}

Our models were informed by the architecture described
by Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2025}, but the implementations
are not identical. Their paper describes dropout and
recurrent dropout within the LSTM layers, whereas our
models use a dropout layer after the recurrent layers.
Our study also extends the comparison to GRU models
and architectures with a bidirectional second layer.

The baseline paper does not specify several training
details, including the optimiser, learning rate, batch
size, training seed, and exact data split. We therefore
used the settings in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}.
Its reported accuracy of 88.40\% is treated as an
external reference rather than a directly reproduced
baseline. The paper does not state a repeated-run
protocol or report uncertainty across training seeds.
We compare its reported score with our repeated-run
averages in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Variation in Test Accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:variance} reports how architecture, activation
choice and training seeds contribute to variation in test
accuracy across the 2{,}000 runs.

Differences between runs of the same configuration account
for \textbf{58.7\%} of the total variance. Architecture accounts
for 26.7\%, including 26.3\% for the choice between LSTM and
GRU and 0.19\% for making the second recurrent layer
bidirectional. Activation pairs account for \textbf{3.7\%}.
The reported seed contribution is about sixteen times the
activation-pair contribution.

\subsection{Agreement Between Single-Seed and Five-Seed Rankings}

We ranked the 100 activation pairs in each architecture
by their original single-run accuracy and by their
five-seed mean. Table~\ref{tab:reproducibility} compares
these rankings.

\input{tables/tab8_reproducibility.tex}

The Spearman correlations are $-0.174$ for BiLSTM+LSTM,
$-0.059$ for BiGRU+GRU, $-0.041$ for BiLSTM+BiLSTM and
$+0.055$ for BiGRU+BiGRU. These values show little agreement
between the rankings. Pairs move 32 to 37 places on average.
In each architecture, at most one pair from the original
top ten remains in the top ten after averaging five runs.

For example, LeakyReLU + Tanh leads the original BiGRU+GRU
ranking at 88.05\%. Its five repeated runs score 86.70\%,
87.13\%, 87.45\%, 87.46\% and 88.20\%. The mean is 87.39\%,
with a standard deviation of 0.55 points, placing it 77th.
ReLU + LeakyReLU has the highest five-seed mean at 87.81\%,
with a standard deviation of 0.17 points
(Table~\ref{tab:top_configs}). The original winners in
BiLSTM+LSTM, BiLSTM+BiLSTM and BiGRU+BiGRU move to
98th, 91st and 85th, respectively.

\subsection{Mean Accuracy for Each Activation Function}

For each dense layer, we average each activation function
over its ten partners and five seeds, giving 50 runs per
mean. In the first dense layer, the spread between these
means is at most 0.67 percentage points across the four
architectures. In BiGRU+GRU, it is 0.18 points.
The means for both dense layers are provided in the
supplementary material.

The six comparisons between architecture rankings give
Spearman correlations from $-0.164$ to $+0.576$. None is
statistically significant at the 5\% level ($p\geq0.082$).
ELU illustrates how rankings change after repeated runs.
In the original first-layer rankings, it ranks first in
BiGRU+GRU and last in BiLSTM+LSTM. In the five-seed
rankings, it ranks ninth and third, respectively.

\subsection{Learning During Training}

To examine early learning, we repeat the ten pairs that
use the same activation in both dense layers (X+X) on
BiGRU+GRU. Each pair is trained with five seeds, with
results recorded after each epoch
(Table~\ref{tab:convergence},
Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence_curves}).

\input{tables/tab9_convergence.tex}

After one epoch, Mish, GELU, SiLU and Hardswish average
\textbf{73.70\%} validation accuracy. ReLU, LeakyReLU and
SELU average \textbf{61.05\%}. The 12.65-point gap is
2.9 times the median standard deviation across seeds
of 4.39 points. The single-seed and five-seed rankings
also show strong agreement
(Spearman $\rho=0.891$, $p=0.0005$).

By epoch three, the validation losses are similar
(Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence_curves}). The early accuracy
difference does not lead to a clear difference in final

\subsection{Variation Between Runs}

The median standard deviation across seeds is 0.30
percentage points for GRU configurations and 0.50 for
LSTM configurations ($p\approx2\times10^{-7}$).
The GRU value is about 40\% lower.

All eight configurations with a standard deviation above
one percentage point use LSTM. Hardswish + LeakyReLU in
BiLSTM+LSTM has the widest spread: its mean is 82.06\%,
its standard deviation is 9.27 points, and its five runs
range from 65.50\% to 86.85\%. No run among the 2{,}000
scores below 60\%.

The largest gap between a configuration's mean precision
and mean recall is 6.89 percentage points. Individual runs
show gaps as large as 24.17 points. Averaging across seeds
therefore gives smaller precision--recall gaps than those
seen in some individual runs.

In BiLSTM+LSTM, ReLU has the highest first-layer mean
accuracy at 86.83\%, compared with 86.60\% across the
other first-layer functions ($p=0.006$).

The reported mean training time is 13.4~s per run on an
NVIDIA RTX 5080. BiGRU+GRU has about 734K parameters,
compared with 764K for BiLSTM+LSTM
(Table~\ref{tab:architectures}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1--RQ3: Architecture, Activation Choice and Training Seeds}

Table~\ref{tab:variance} shows that training seeds account for
58.7\% of the variation in test accuracy, compared with 26.7\%
for architecture and 3.7\% for activation pairs. Variation
between training runs is therefore larger than the variation
linked to either architecture or activation choice alone.

The classification results in Section~\ref{sec:results} show
a 0.79-percentage-point gap between GRU and LSTM, with Cohen's
$d=1.19$. Table~\ref{tab:sweep_summary} shows a smaller change
from making the second recurrent layer bidirectional: LSTM
accuracy rises from 86.62\% to 86.75\%, while both GRU
architectures average 87.48\% at the reported precision.
Layer-2 bidirectionality accounts for 0.19\% of the total
variance (Table~\ref{tab:variance}).

Within BiGRU+GRU, the mean accuracies of the 100 activation
pairs range from 87.12\% to 87.81\%
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep_summary}). This 0.69-point spread is
smaller than the 0.79-point gap between GRU and LSTM.
The same table reports a median standard deviation of
0.33 points across seeds. Thus, differences between
activation pairs occur alongside variation between training
runs, which limits how clearly small accuracy gaps can
be interpreted.
\subsection{RQ4: Agreement Between Single-Seed and Five-Seed Rankings}

The two rankings show little agreement in all four architectures
(Table~\ref{tab:reproducibility}). Their Spearman correlations range
from $-0.174$ to $+0.055$, all close to zero. Activation pairs move
32 to 37 places on average between the rankings. In each
architecture, at most one pair from the original top ten remains
in the top ten after averaging five runs.

The small accuracy gaps between pairs help explain these changes.
Across architectures, the median standard deviation across seeds
ranges from 0.29 to 0.53 percentage points. When accuracy gaps are
small relative to this variation, changes between training runs
can change the order of the pairs. Here, the ranking from one run
gives little indication of the ranking based on five runs.

\subsection{RQ5--RQ6: Findings Across Seeds and Estimated Seed Counts}
\label{sec:rq6}

The clearest activation difference appears after the first epoch.
Mish, GELU, SiLU and Hardswish average 73.70\% validation accuracy,
compared with 61.05\% for ReLU, LeakyReLU and SELU. The 12.65-point
gap is 2.9 times the median standard deviation across seeds.
The single-seed and five-seed rankings are also similar
($\rho=0.891$). This difference concerns early learning; final
accuracy shows no clear separation. GRU models vary less between
runs than LSTM models. All eight configurations with a standard
deviation above one percentage point use LSTM.
The estimated seed counts depend on the size of the accuracy gap.
Using the median standard deviation of 0.38 percentage points,
80\% power and $\alpha=0.05$, the 0.79-point gap between GRU and
LSTM corresponds to about \textbf{4} seeds per configuration.
The 0.12-point gap between the first- and tenth-ranked
configurations corresponds to about \textbf{157}. These estimates
are based on the observed variation. At 13.4~s per run, 157 seeds
across 100 activation pairs correspond to about 58 GPU-hours
for one architecture.

\subsection{Limitations}

The study covers one dataset and one task family, so the variance
split should be re-estimated before being assumed elsewhere. The
data split is held fixed by design, so our estimates exclude split
variance and are lower bounds. Five seeds suffice for the
architectural conclusions, but leave per-cell intervals wide
relative to the differences between cells, so individual cell means
should be read with their intervals attached.

\section{Conclusion}

We trained 2{,}000 models, testing 100 activation pairs across
four recurrent architectures with five seeds each. Training
seeds accounted for 58.7\% of the variation in test accuracy,
compared with 26.7\% for architecture and 3.7\% for activation
pairs.

Our main contribution is showing how repeated training changes
the conclusions of an activation comparison. Earlier studies
discussed in this paper reported rankings from single runs.
In our experiments, those rankings showed little agreement
with five-run rankings, moving more than 32 places on average.
This challenges the use of a single run as evidence that one
activation pair is better than another.

Architecture differences were clearer. GRU exceeded LSTM by
0.79 percentage points, supplied all fifty highest-scoring
configurations, and showed about 40\% lower standard deviation
across seeds. Making the second GRU layer bidirectional added
no measurable accuracy benefit.

Activation choice mainly affected early learning. Mish, GELU,
SiLU and Hardswish averaged 73.70\% validation accuracy after
one epoch, compared with 61.05\% for ReLU, LeakyReLU and SELU.
Their early advantage did not produce a clear difference in
final test accuracy.

Under our IMDb settings, architecture choice deserves more
attention than small differences between activation pairs.
More broadly, claims of better performance should be supported
by repeated runs and reported uncertainty. Future work should
test these findings on other datasets, different data splits,
and attention-based models.
